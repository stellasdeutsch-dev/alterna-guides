To whom, where to, and toward what

In English we say: give it to a friend. Go home. Write to Mom. Look at the cat.

Four phrases. Two different prepositions, plus one with none at all.

Turkish looks at this and says: why so complicated? It takes one suffix. Slaps it on the end of the word. Done. Even “home” gets it, where English has nothing: eve.

The dative works like an arrow. Not where you’re standing. Where the action is flying.

Guess before the rule: ev means house. How do you say “home” as in “going home”?

01

Which tail? Let’s ring it up

There are four tails: -a, -e, -ya, -ye. The word itself decides which one. Pick a word and the receipt adds it up.

  • Last vowel. a, ı, o, u → -a. e, i, ö, ü → -e.
  • Final vowel. Turkish doesn’t like two vowels in a row, so the buffer y steps in.
  • p, ç, t, k at the end love to soften: kitap → kitaba.
  • A proper name goes behind an apostrophe: Ankara'ya.

Say it: çocuğa ≈ “cho-joo-ah” (ç = ch, c = j in jam, ğ is silent). kediye ≈ “keh-dee-yeh.” ı sounds like the a in about.

03

Where to. The easiest one

Direction of movement. You walk, ride, fly. Turkish doesn’t care how. It cares where to.

Eve gidiyorum.I’m going home.
Okula gidiyoruz.We’re going to school.
İstanbul'a uçuyorum.I’m flying to Istanbul.
Restorana gidiyor.He’s going to the restaurant.

With names of cities, countries, and people, the suffix goes behind an apostrophe. The apostrophe is a fence. Leave the name behind it alone.

Where to or where at? Switch

Eve gidiyorum.I’m going home.
✕English “at” and “to” don’t map neatly. Mix up -e and -de, and instead of “I’m going home” you get “I’m going while being at home.” Like on a treadmill.
04

To whom. The receiver of the action

You write, give, ask, help. Whoever receives it goes in the dative.

Sana mesaj yazıyorum.I’m writing you a message.
Ayşe'ye çiçek alıyorum.I’m buying Ayşe flowers.
Öğretmene soru soruyorum.I’m asking the teacher a question.
Murat'a yardım ediyorum.I’m helping Murat.

Pronouns. Tap to flip

Ben doesn’t become “bene.” Turkish took pity on us and shortened it to bana. Sen became sana. Learn these two by sight, like English I → me. In ona there’s a buffer n.

Bana çay veriyor.He’s giving me tea.
Ona inanıyorum.I believe him (or her).
05

Toward what. Where your attention points

The dative isn’t just for people and places. It marks anything your gaze, trust, or effort reaches toward. English uses at, on, with — or nothing. Turkish uses one tail. Switch and see.

The big idea. A Turkish verb decides for itself which case it needs. The English preposition won’t help: “look at,” “get on,” “agree with,” “help” with nothing — all dative in Turkish. Learn each verb together with its tail.

06

Questions. Nereye, kime, neye

The question is in the dative too. You answer in the same case. Handy. English leaves “to” hanging at the end: “Who are you writing to?” Turkish sticks it on the question word: Kime?

Is the action flying toward a person, a place, a thing, or an idea? Turkish almost certainly wants the dative. Ignore the English word: to, at, on, with, or nothing.
