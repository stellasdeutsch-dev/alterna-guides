    var
    Rule · sheet 03

    Place, thing, var

    A Turk builds a phrase like an architect: first the lot, then the house, then the sign “there is” or “there isn’t.” The blocks always stand in the same order. Rearrange them — and the house collapses. English builds from the roof down: There’s a table in the room. Turkish starts with the lot: Odada masa var.

    1 · the lot

    Tap the blocks: change the place, the thing and the sign. The place takes -da from the last topic. The thing is as in the dictionary. At the end — var or yok.

    Compare · sheet 04

    Is, are — var

    Good news first. English keeps the noun as it is: a table, no table. Turkish too: masa stays masa. And Turkish has it even easier: one var for “there is” and “there are.”

    English

    There’s a table in the room.

    Turkish

    Odada masa var.

    English

    There are two chairs.

    Turkish

    İki sandalye var.

    Flip the switch. English rebuilds the sentence: a → no, are two → aren’t any. Turkish swaps one word at the end: var → yok. That’s the easy part. The hard part — the order — is on the next sheets.

    mı?
    Question · sheet 05

    Var mı? — Var.

    Odada kedi var mı?

    English flips the words: there is → is there? Turkish moves nothing. It keeps the phrase and adds a small word at the end: mı after var, mu after yok. That’s vowel harmony. The particle is written apart.

    var mı?a → ı
    yok mu?o → u

    ı without a dot is its own letter: say it like the a in about. So mı is not “me.” If the question already has ne (“what”), no particle: Odada ne var?

    Answers are short

    One word works too: Var. or Yok. That’s what Turks do. The landlord says “var” fast, and “yok” after a pause, with a sigh.

    English ↔ Turkish · sheet 05b

    Read it backwards

    English puts “there is” first and the place last. Turkish does the mirror: place first, var last. And the little “a” just falls off — Turkish needs no article here.

    Tap a sentence. Watch the words swap places. The question works the same: “is” runs to the end and turns into mı.

    Fine point · sheet 06

    Camera and crosshairs

    Two similar phrases shoot different movies. One looks for a thing, the other surveys a place. English marks this with the and a: The book is on the table vs There’s a book on the table. Turkish has no articles. It uses word order instead.

    Left camera: “the book” — known, so it comes first, no var. Right camera: “a book” — new, so the place comes first and var closes.

    where?
    Kitap masada.

    The book is on the table. We know which book and we’re looking for it.

    what’s there?
    Masada kitap var.

    There’s a book on the table. We look at the table and list what’s on it.

    “Where’s the cat? — The cat is on the balcony”

    Meaning trap · sheet 07

    Three different “no”s

    English “no” does two jobs: it answers a question (No, I don’t) and it says something is missing (no coffee). Turkish splits them: hayır answers, yok means “none here.” And “is not” is a third word, değil. Three doors. Open all three.

    Hayır — you answer a question. Değil — you argue with a label. Yok — you point at an empty shelf.

    Hayır“no” as an answer to a questionHayır, yok.No, there isn’t.
    Değil“is not”: this isn’t coffeeBu kahve değil, çay.This isn’t coffee, it’s tea.
    Yok“there’s none”: no coffeeEvde kahve yok.There’s no coffee at home.
    “There’s no coffee at home”

      Traps · sheet 10

      Four traps

      These are English-shaped mistakes: the words are Turkish, the habits are English. Tap “fix” — the drawing gets corrected.

      Var bir masa odada.Odada masa var.

      That’s “There is a table in the room,” word by word. Turkish: place first, var last, no “a.”

      Var mı odada kedi?Odada kedi var mı?

      English flips: “Is there…?” Turkish doesn’t. Same order, mı at the end.

      Evde hayır kahve.Evde kahve yok.

      “No coffee” isn’t an answer to a question. Hayır only answers. Missing things are yok.

      Odada üç sandalyeler var.Odada üç sandalye var.

      English needs “chairs.” Turkish doesn’t: the number already said it.
