Guess first: what’s the difference between these two sentences about the café?

On the left, Timur stood at the door and saw the lock. Kapalıydı — it was closed. The witness voice.

On the right, he only read it in the app. Kapalıymış — closed, they say. Hearsay.

The third case is a surprise. Timur pushed the door and it gave: Aa, açıkmış! Turns out it’s open.

Rule: -(y)mış means “turns out,” “they say,” “apparently.” You didn’t see it, or you only just saw it.

English has no ending for this. You add words: “turns out,” “apparently,” “they say.” Turkish packs all of it into one suffix.

How does Timur know? Pick one — and the sentence changes.

The word’s last vowel picks the ending. Like a taxi picks the fare by the route.

a, ı → -mış. e, i → -miş. Hava güzelmiş — the weather is great, turns out.

o, u → -muş. ö, ü → -müş. Four fares. Say it right: ş = sh, ı = the a in “about.”

Does the word end in a vowel? Add y, like in hastaydı: hasta → hastaymış.

Meğer= turns out

Evening. Timur fell asleep in the taxi and woke up by the sea. Meğer çok yorgunmuşum. Turns out I was really tired.

Meğer means “turns out.” This word likes to go around with -mış.

After -mış we add the person: -um, -sun, -uz, -sunuz, -lar. For “he/she” — nothing. In English “I” says who. In Turkish the ending does.

With “I,” -mış is a discovery about yourself. You didn’t notice, and now it’s dawned on you.

Timur meets Mert. Turns out Mert is a teacher. How do you exclaim it?

There is and there isn’t: varmış, yokmuş. Kafede wifi yokmuş — no Wi-Fi, they say.

Değil = “is not.” Yok = “there is no.” English makes the same split. Same pair as değildi and yoktu.

The office again. Timur is writing a report for the boss. The key question: did you see this?

Saw it yourself — -ydı. Found out from a message — -ymış, even when English says “was.” Timur hasn’t seen Ahmet today.

Same with verbs: gelmemiş — “didn’t come, they say.” Here -mış sits on a noun or adjective.

Difference · English ↔ Turkish

English adds words. Turkish adds -mış.

Same idea: I didn’t see it, I heard it, or it just hit me. But English needs three or four extra words. Turkish needs one ending.

  1. Words ↔ ending.“Turns out,” “apparently,” “they say” — all of it is -mış, glued to the end of the word.
  2. No “is.”Turkish has no “to be” here. Ahmet hastaymış = Ahmet + sick + turns-out.
  3. “Is” or “was”? Turkish doesn’t care.English must pick. Hastaymış can be “he’s sick” or “he was sick.” Context decides.
  4. The ending says who.English needs “I.” In Turkish -ım does that job: hastaymışım. Ben is optional.

English · 4–5 words
