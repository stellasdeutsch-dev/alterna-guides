The ownership mechanism

Two parts — and any Turk knows whose it is. The owner gets one tail, the thing gets another. Build the mechanism right here. Without them, a Turk only gets that there’s a house somewhere. Whose — a mystery.

A taxi to Mehmet’s

Beşiktaş. Evening. You get into a taxi. You need to get to your friend Mehmet’s.

03rule

Point A and point B

Point A is the owner. It gets the tail -(n)In. Point B is the thing. It gets -(s)I, like “his” from the last topic.

Point Aowner · whose?-(n)In
Point Bthing · what?-(s)I

Remember the picture: the owner rings, the thing opens. One part isn’t enough. Ali'nin without evi is a ring into the void. Evi without Ali'nin is a door opened for who knows whom.

04infographic

Eight tails

The vowel in the owner’s tail follows four-way harmony: ı, i, u, ü. A word ending in a vowel adds n. Tap a slice of the chart.

An owner ending in a consonant takes the short tail: kızın, evin. One ending in a vowel takes it with a prop: arabanın, kedinin. The harmony is the same one you already know.

05names

The apostrophe bouncer

Names of people, cities, and countries are capitalized. Their tail gets in through an apostrophe, like a bouncer at a club door. Plain words walk in without one: kedinin.

Ali'nin

The apostrophe doesn’t change the sound. You read it as one word.

06pronouns

Two rebels

Pronouns can be owners too. Four behave. Two rebel: ben and biz take -im, not -in. Tap a card.

The rebels are sneaky: they look almost like everyone else. One letter — and you’re one of them.

The thing adjusts to the owner: benim evim, senin evin, onun evi. You can already do this.

07compare

Four languages

Russian puts the thing first and the owner last. Turkish flips it: owner first. English does the same, only it has one tail.

Kazakh is the best friend here: both words get a tail, same order.

08props

Two props

Each part has its own prop. An owner ending in a vowel takes n. A thing ending in a vowel takes s. Consonants don’t need a prop.

owner
thing
ns

The two props are easy to mix up. Remember it like this: n stands by the doorbell, s stands by the door. Kedinin odası — both in place.

09order

Swap them

The owner always comes first. Swap the words — and you swap the meaning. The girl’s mom turns into the mom’s daughter. Tap and see.

Thinking “the house of Ali” hurts here: you want to start with the house. A Turk starts with the owner.

Asking about the owner is easy: Kimin? — whose? Kimin evi? — whose house? Answer: Ali'nin.

10fares

Three levels of precision

There are different ways to say “whose.” Pick a fare: the higher it is, the less confusion. In a taxi you need VIP only. The other fares work when everyone already knows who you mean.

11algorithm

Three questions

Ask them in order. The dot runs along the chart and builds the pair. Pick the parts yourself and see if your answer matches the dot.

  1. Who’s the owner? A pronoun has its own form. A name goes through an apostrophe.
  2. Does the owner end in a vowel? Then the tail comes with the prop n.
  3. Does the thing end in a vowel? Then the tail comes with the prop s.
12mnemonic

The doorbell

The owner rings the doorbell: “nin!” The thing opens: “i!” Nin — i. A ring with no answer is pointless; a door with no ring won’t open.

-nin-i

A doorbell without a door is a prank. A door without a doorbell is a draft.

Whose Istanbul?

Every district has something of its own. Tap the red rings on the map. Each pair is a ready-made izafet. Read it out loud: that’s how your ear gets used to the “nin — i” rhythm.
