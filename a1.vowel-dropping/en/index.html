The vowel has left the chat

Istanbul. A pharmacy in Kadıköy. Your nose is stuffed. You point at it and speak with confidence. The pharmacist nods and answers. Listen closely.

9:41Eczane
Merhaba. Burunum ağrıyor.“Hello. My nose hurts.”
Burnunuz mu?“Your nose?”
Evet, burunum.“Yes, my nose.”
Anladım. Burnunuz ağrıyor.“Got it. Your nose hurts.”

Where’s the mistake? What would a Turk say?

You took “nose,” added “my,” done. That’s how English works: the word never changes. Turkish disagrees. The pharmacist was polite: corrected you and didn’t laugh. Almost.

One letter left. Quietly. Like a guest who felt cramped. Today we figure out who leaves and when.

Rule

Three conditions. All at once.

Two syllables

A short word: bu·run, a·ğız, i·sim.

A high vowel at the end

The second syllable has ı, i, u or ü.

A suffix starting with a vowel

“My,” “your,” “his/her” are glued on: -ım, -ın, -ı.

A high vowel sits between two consonants. Another vowel arrives. It’s crowded. Someone has to leave. The high one leaves: you could barely hear it anyway. The ı in burun sounds like the a in “about” — short and lazy.

Face and body

Let’s start with the face. Here everyone drops.

Tap a body part. Switch between “my” and “his/her.” Turkish has one word for he and she: o. That’s why this topic lives in a pharmacy.

From the textbook: Ayşe'nin boynu ağrıyor. Ayşe’s neck hurts.

Not just the body

Six more words that slim down

The list is short: the body and a few smart words. Name, city, son, mind, photo, thought. No rule tells you which words are on it. Learn it like the menu at your favorite coffee shop. Tap a card: it shows what’s left of the word.

English ↔ Turkish

English does it too. Rarely.

Hunger → hungry. Not hungery. Same trick: a weak vowel between two consonants, a vowel arrives, the weak one leaves. Pick a group and a pair: English on top, Turkish below.

Every day, not once a year

English drops a vowel in a few word pairs. Turkish does it every time you say “my,” “your,” “his/her.”

Turkish spells what it says

You say “choc-late” and “fam-ly” but write every letter. Turkish writes burnum, exactly as it sounds.

“My” is glued on

“My nose” is two words. burnum is one: burn-um. The glue is what squeezes the vowel out.

Lab

Build it yourself. Watch who leaves.

Pick a word and a suffix. The high vowel either drops or stays. The third tab has the tricky ones.

Traps

English speakers trip here. That’s normal.

“benim burun”?

In English “my” is enough: my nose. So you put benim in front and leave the noun alone.

benim burnum. The ending is a must, benim is optional. Just burnum is fine.

School doesn’t slim down

okul: two syllables, high u. Everything fits. And nothing drops.

okul → okulum. Like hungry but watery: some words are on the list, some aren’t. Memorize the list.

“burnı”?

The vowel left, so you forget harmony. The ending is picked by the last vowel of the word.

burun → burnu, boyun → boynu, göğüs → göğsü. The u left, but its echo stays in the ending.

When it drops

Two syllables, a high vowel in the second, a suffix starting with a vowel. Like hunger → hungry.

ağzım · burnum · boynum

Face and body: alnım, göğsüm, karnım.

ismim · şehrim · oğlum

Also: aklım, resmim, fikrim.

Doesn’t drop

burunlar, okulum, kalemim. Consonant suffix, word not on the list, low vowel. Harmony stays: burnu, not burnı.
