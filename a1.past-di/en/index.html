The past in -DI is the witness tense. You were there, took part, did it yourself. And you stand by your words.

Four vowels

The tail copies the root

Look: geldi, aldı, okudu, gördü. Notice anything?

The tail has four vowels: ı, i, u, ü. Which one shows up depends on the root’s last vowel. Same harmony as in mı, mi, mu, mü.

No need to cram a table. Listen to the root: whatever vowel sounded last echoes in the tail. Tap a verb — the wheel finds the vowel by itself.

a, ı → ıe, i → io, u → uö, ü → ü
Devoicing machine

Fıstıkçı Şahap is back

You learned geldi, then saw gitti and yaptı. Where did the “t” come from? Who put it there?

Remember the pistachio seller? His name holds f, s, t, k, ç, ş, h, p. If the root ends in one of them, d goes voiceless and becomes t.

+ di=

Why

Say “yapdı” out loud. Your tongue trips. Voiceless p and voiced d don’t live side by side.

Turkish doesn’t like awkward, so it devoices. You get yaptı. You’d say it that way yourself if you didn’t think about it.

Two t’s in a row are fine

gitti: one t from the root git, the other from the tail. sattı — sold, same story.

Builder

Build the conjugation

The bricks always go in one order: root + d/t with a vowel + person.

The person endings here are short: m, n, nothing, k, nız, lar. “He” has an empty tail: geldi — he came.

The vowel in the person ending obeys the root too: geldiniz, okudunuz, gördünüz. And “they” works without -lar: onlar geldi. Turks often say it that way.
