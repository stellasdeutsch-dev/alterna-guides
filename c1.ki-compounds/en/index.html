Fact → conclusion

Guess first. I call Mert. It rings. He doesn’t pick up. What follows from that?

ki on its own links two ideas: Duydum ki gidiyorsun, “I heard that you’re leaving.” Today ki sticks to other words.

First pair. Fact on the left, conclusion on the right: Mert telefonu açmıyor, demek ki kızgın.

demek ki means “so.” First what you see. Then what follows from it.

I text Deniz. She’s nicer than Mert, answers faster, and always looks for a way out.

Deniz: Geliriz, yeter ki sen bizi karşıla. “We’ll come, as long as you pick us up.”

yeter ki means “as long as, if only.” Literally “it’s enough that.” One condition, and everything’s still on.

After yeter ki comes a wish or a request: karşıla, gelin, olsun.

I answer right away: Her şeyi ayarlarım, yeter ki gelin.

Finish it: Her şeye razıyım, yeter ki deniz…

Scene 4 · breakdown

Besides

I rewind my message. I was sure kaldı ki meant “but at least.” It sounds like “what’s left,” after all.

But kaldı ki means “besides, on top of that.” The second argument lands on the same side of the scale.

The hotel is pricey. Besides, the sea is cold. Both weights are against the trip.

“But at least” in Turkish is ama: Otel pahalı ama deniz var. The scale balances out.

And kaldı ki can play on my side too, if the argument is right: Gelin, kaldı ki 19 Mayıs'ta bayram var.

Which sentence invites the friends?

The chat comes alive. Elif has already drawn up a schedule: by the hour, with an emergency exit.

Elif: Planım hazır, şöyle ki: sabah Amisos Tepesi, akşam Batıpark.

şöyle ki means “namely, here’s the thing.” Details follow, often after a colon.

Mert won’t give up: Tut ki yağmur yağdı. Ne yapacağız?

tut ki means “say, suppose.” It builds an imagined situation. A question or plan B follows.

Mert has thawed. He writes long, almost solemn, like a vacation postcard.

Nasıl ki biz sana İstanbul'u gösterdik, sen de bize Samsun'u göstereceksin.

nasıl ki means “just as.” The second half answers like a mirror, usually with de.

I answer without thinking: Bafra pidesini illaki yiyeceksiniz. “You’re definitely eating Bafra pide.”

illaki means “definitely, like it or not.” Written as one word. The other ki conjunctions are written apart.
