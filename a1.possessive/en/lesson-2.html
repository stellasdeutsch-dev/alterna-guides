harmony

Four or two

“Our” and “your” follow four-way harmony: ı, i, u, ü. “Their” follows two-way harmony: a or e. Inside sits the familiar lar, and it doesn’t know any other way.

Like two elevators in one building. One goes to four floors, the other only to two.

for emphasis

The spotlight word

You can put an owner word in front of the thing: benim, bizim. That’s a spotlight: “mine, not anyone else’s.” The ending doesn’t go anywhere.

benim

Your English brain wants to say benim ev and call it a day. Won’t work: without the tail, the thing belongs to nobody again. A spotlight without a stamp proves nothing.

All six spotlights work the same way: onun evi, sizin eviniz, onların evleri.

etiquette

Polite “you”

A coffee shop in Beşiktaş. The barista writes names on cups. He’ll ask you Adınız?, not Adın?. To a stranger it’s the polite “your,” even if it’s just one person.

In a store, a taxi, a bank — always polite. The mistake won’t kill you. It just sounds a bit too homey.

Adınız?

To a stranger — the polite “your”: adınız.

You answer about yourself, of course: Adım Anna. That’s “my,” no politeness needed.

algorithm

Three questions, six answers

The algorithm from lesson one, just with more owners. The dot runs along the chart and builds the form.

  1. Who’s the owner? We — mız, you all — nız, they — ları.
  2. How does the word end? After a consonant, “our” and “your” need a linking vowel.
  3. What’s the last vowel? For “their,” the a or e pair is enough.
mnemonic

M is ours, N is yours, ları is theirs

Turkish ız sounds a lot like English “is.” Ours is m + “is.” Yours (all of you) is n + “is.” And “theirs” takes the whole lot: ları.

-mız-nız-ları

One owner — one letter. A group — the letter plus “is.” Silly? You won’t forget it, though.
