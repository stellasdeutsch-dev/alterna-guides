Three letters. -tir. The difference between “I got a haircut” and “I butchered my own hair.”

A Turkish verb always snitches on who did the work. You, or someone at your request. That's the causative.

02 · floors

One verb

English adds extra words around the verb: cut, have it cut, get it cut. Turkish takes one verb and stacks floors on it. No helper verbs, no word order games.

Read it from the bottom up: root, order, tense, person.

The order always sits right on the root. Tense and person come after it.

I: which vowel?

I in the suffix is ı, i, u or ü, by the last vowel of the root. D is d or t: after voiceless f, s, t, k, ç, ş, h, p it's t. That's why yap → yaptır, but yaz → yazdır.

There are short stubborn ones with their own suffixes. They're waiting in lesson two.

04 · English ↔ Türkçe

Extra words

English builds the causative out of extra words: have it done, get it done, make someone do it. Sometimes it even swaps the verb: die → kill. Turkish does all of that with a few letters inside one verb. Pick a pair and watch the extra words melt into the suffix.

06 · traps

Where people trip

Four mistakes that sound fine to an English ear. Tap to see the right way.

Berberde saçımı kestim.

In English, “I cut my hair” often means the barber did it. In Turkish, kestim means your own hands and your own scissors.

Saçımı kestirdim.
Beni bir saat bekledi.

You meant “She kept me waiting for an hour.” But bekledi is “she waited for me.” The “kept me waiting” part is one t.

Beni bir saat bekletti.
Patron bizi çalışmak yaptı.

“Make” is not yapmak here. Don’t build it word by word, like in English. The “make” lives inside the verb: çalış + tır.

Patron bizi çalıştırdı.
yapdır

English spelling keeps d no matter what. Turkish writes what you hear: after voiceless p, D turns into t.
