One forum. Two pasts.

Picture this: a minister is coming to Izmir. To a big forum.

You're a volunteer, helping with the organizing. You see everything with your own eyes. Your mom, in another city, learns about the forum from the news.

For you it's geldi. For Mom — gelmiş. One minister. Different grammar.

    Switch and see what changes. Only the tail. Same words.

    Look at the last line. Mom is talking about you, but also with -mış: she learned it from TV.

    Mom isn't lying or doubting. She's just being honest: I wasn't there, I found out.

    01 / negation

    Negation: -me- right after the root

    Guess first: how do you say “they say he didn't come”?

    Life hack. After me it's always miş. After ma it's always mış. The tail takes its vowel from the negation, not the root. Tap a root.

    Look at oku: not okumamuş, but okumamış. Four tails shrink to two.

    English puts “not” in a separate word before the verb: she didn't come. Turkish hides it inside the verb, right after the root: gel-me-miş.

    Compare with -dı. Gelmedim — I didn't come, I know it. Gelmemişim — turns out I didn't come. Say, you mixed up the meeting day.

    02 / question

    Question: the ending moves out

    With -dı the particle mi goes after the ending: geldin mi?

    With -mış it's the other way around. The ending moves onto the particle: gelmiş misin?

    English flips the order: “Did you come?” Turkish never flips anything. It just adds mi at the end.

    Between two vowels the buffer y steps in: miyim, miyiz. Only onlar is stubborn: gelmişler mi? Its ending stays home.

    A -mış question sounds like an English tag question: “So you moved, did you?” It's a soft way to check a rumor.

    The answer usually comes with -dı: Evet, geldim. You do know whether you came or not.

    English ↔ Turkish

    No “did,” no “not.” Just tails.

    English builds questions and negatives with extra words: did, didn't. Turkish adds pieces to the verb and never changes the word order.

      04 / fairy tale

      Bir varmış, bir yokmuş

      This is how Turkish fairy tales begin. Literally “there was one, there wasn't one” — the Turkish “once upon a time.” English tales use the plain past: there was a king. Turkish tales use -mış to the very end. Nobody saw the tale. It gets retold.

      -mış sticks even to var: varmış — “there was, they say.” Flip through the book and tap the lines.

      A fairy tale keeps one tail to the very end. If -dı suddenly pops up, the narrator was there in person. That doesn't happen in fairy tales.

      Bir varmış, bir yokmuş.Once upon a time. Literally: there was, there wasn't.
      Evvel zaman içinde bir padişah varmış.Long ago there lived a padishah.
      Padişahın üç kızı varmış.The padishah had three daughters.
      Bir gün küçük kız ormana gitmiş.One day the youngest daughter went into the forest.
      Orada bir kurt görmüş.There she saw a wolf.
      Kurt ona «Merhaba!» demiş.The wolf said to her: “Hello!”
