Turkish reads the way it’s written. Almost.

Kadıköy. A café. You sit by the window.

There’s a menu on the wall. First line — ÇAY.

The waiter comes over. You say, kay. Like “okay.”

The waiter says, kay?

You say, yeah. Kay. One.

He says, çay. And brings tea.

There was a tail hanging under the C. Tiny. That’s what turned “kay” into tea. You know this word, by the way. It’s chai.

Guess first. How do you read ÇİÇEK?

Stop 1 · 29 letters

The Latin alphabet. Almost familiar.

Turkish has 29 letters. One letter — one sound. It reads the way it’s written.

English can’t do that. There you write “though” and “tough” and read them differently. Not here. And no q, w or x. None at all.

Most letters do what you expect. A few familiar ones have a new job. They’re blue below.

Stop 2 · Accessories

The Latin alphabet went shopping

And bought four accessories. Each one changes the sound. Always the same way.

Tail underneath
c→c

A tail means it hisses.

c “j” (jam) → ç “ch” (church): çay “chai”

s “s” → ş “sh” (ship): şeker “sheh-ker”, sugar

Two dots
o→o

Dots — round your lips. English has no such sounds. Try it in a mirror.

o → ö “e” (bed) with lips round, like for “o”: göz “göz”, eye

u → ü “ee” (see) with lips round, like for “oo”: üç “üch”, three

No dot
i→ı

Take the dot off and you get “uh.” Close to the a in “about.” Keep your lips flat.

i “ee” → ı “uh”: ışık “uh-shuhk”, light

kız “kuhz”, girl

Little hat
g→g

With a hat, the letter goes silent. It just stretches the vowel before it.

dağ “daa”, mountain · ağaç “aach”, tree

The tail hisses. Dots round your lips. No dot — “uh.” A hat — silence.

Stop 4 · Vowels

Eight vowels. Four pairs.

Vowels come in pairs. Top row — no dots. Bottom row — with dots.

The a and e pair breaks the rules: e has no dots. Well, it happens.

One English habit to drop: sliding. English “go” is really “goh-oo,” and “day” is “deh-ee.” Turkish vowels don’t slide. o is just o. e is just e. Hold one sound.

Remember this square. In the next topic it starts bossing the endings around.

Stop 6 · In the city

Kadıköy pier

A departure board. Ferries. You read the names out loud. Yourself. Then tap and check.

VAPURSAAT

Read it first. Then tap the line.

The ticket window. The cashier says, Buyurun. That’s “go ahead.”

You say, Eminönü, lütfen. That’s “please.”

He says, Eminönü? You say, evet. That’s “yes.”

He nods. The token is yours. No “sorry, what?”

The ferry was seven minutes late, by the way. But that’s not about the alphabet.
