Hook

An alibi in Konyaaltı

You rent an apartment in Konyaaltı. Sea view. If you lean out of the balcony up to your waist.

In the morning the downstairs neighbor calls. Soaked, like he just survived a storm. Says water's coming through his ceiling. You say you're sorry. He says it's been going since last night.

An hour later a message arrives. Just one. Short as a verdict. Answer it yourself.

The difference — three letters. Uyudum and uyuyordum. The price — someone else's ceiling.

By the end of the lesson you'll pick this form without thinking. The neighbor will write again, by the way.

Rule

Brush plus stamp

You already know both pieces. -iyor — “going on now.” -dı — “happened.” Glue them — you get “was going on back then.”

gel — the stem: “come.”

Geliyordum. I was coming (right at that moment).

Where does -du come from? It's the old idi — “was.” Scroll: the two words will squeeze into one.

geliyor idim

geliyor idim → geliyordum

The order is iron: stem, -iyor, -du, person. Not one step aside.

The stress falls on -yor: geliYORdum. Same as in the present tense. Nothing to relearn here.

Persons

Six persons, one vowel

After -yor it's always u. Always. Harmony takes a break here: -yor has an o, and after o comes u.

Alıyordum, gülüyordum, geliyordum. Same tail for everyone.

For onlar there are two options: geliyorlardı and geliyordular. You'll hear the first one more often.

The endings are the same as with -dı: -m, -n, nothing, -k, -nuz. You already know them. They just moved in after -yordu.

Review

How -iyor attaches to the stem

You covered this with the present tense. Nothing new. Just a reminder so you don't trip.

Pick a stem

Consonant at the end — the vowel follows harmony: geliyor, alıyor. Vowel a or e at the end — it narrows: bekliyor, başlıyor.

Ye and de are special: yiyor, diyor. And git turns into gidiyor.

The point: all of this happens before -du. The stamp doesn't care what went on with the stem. It just goes on top.

Forms

Negative and question

Negation goes before -yor: gelmiyordum. The wide me narrows to mi here.

The question cuts the word in half. Mu goes after -yor and takes the tail: geliyor muydun?

A y slips in between u and u. Muydun, not muudun. Otherwise your tongue gets tangled.

You can stack negation and question. Gelmiyor muydun? — weren't you coming? That's how you ask when surprised.

Beni dinliyor muydun?Were you listening to me?
Kimse gülmüyordu.Nobody was laughing.
Uyuyor muydunuz?Were you sleeping? (did I wake you?)
Bize gelmiyor muydun?Weren't you coming to see us?
Meaning 1

Freeze-frame

Picture yesterday as a video. Hit pause. Eight a.m. What was going on in that frame? That's -yordu.

When it started and when it ended doesn't matter. What matters is that it was going on at that moment.

Time clues: saat sekizde, dün bu saatte, o sırada. See one of these — reach for -yordu.

“What were you doing at eight?” gets asked by cops, moms and downstairs neighbors. The answer always has the same form.

Meaning 2

Background and event

-yordu paints the background. One long line. -dı stamps: bang — it happened.

Brush first, then the stamp. You were in the shower — the phone rang.

If both verbs are stamps, it's a chain: came, saw, called. No background.

The test is simple. Can you add “at that time”? If yes — you're looking at a brush.

Meanings 3–5

Three more jobs

Two brushes side by side. Nobody interrupts anybody. Both were just busy. Bliss.

Ben yemek yapıyordum, o televizyon izliyordu.I was cooking, and he was watching TV.
Ayşe çay içiyordu, ben ödev yapıyordum.Ayşe was drinking tea, I was doing homework.

With words like eskiden, geçen yaz, -yordu tells how life was back then. It's not like that anymore. English uses “used to” for this.

Eskiden her sabah denize gidiyordum.I used to go to the sea every morning.
Geçen yaz Antalya'da yaşıyordum.Last summer I was living in Antalya.
Eskiden Türkçe bilmiyordum.I didn't use to know Turkish.

With az kalsın and neredeyse, -yordu means “almost.” The process started — and didn't happen. Lucky you.

English tricks you here: “I almost fell” makes you want düştüm. But düştüm means you're already on the floor.

Az kalsın otobüsü kaçırıyordum.I almost missed the bus.
Neredeyse düşüyordum.I almost fell.
Comparison

English and Turkish

English has “did” and “was doing.” Looks like -dı and -yordu. But it's not one-to-one.

At nine I was watching a movie.?
Yesterday I watched a movie. (the whole thing)?
I used to watch a lot of TV shows.?

“Yesterday I watched a movie” is a fact. The whole thing. A Turk says izledim. “At nine I was watching” is a freeze-frame. That's izliyordum. And “used to watch” — English switches forms, Turkish stays with izliyordum.

In English: I was reading — “was” plus -ing. Turkish glued it into one word — okuyordum.
