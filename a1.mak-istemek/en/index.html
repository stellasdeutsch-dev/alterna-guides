scroll 03 · thing or action

Want something — or want to do something?

Want a thing — name the thing. Then istiyorum. Want an action — a verb steps in between.

çayiçmekistiyorum
I want to drink tea.
simityemekistiyorum
I want to eat a simit.
biletalmakistiyorum
I want to buy a ticket.

A thing — you need an object. An action — you need a process. The waiter cares about the difference.

You can want a simit. You can want to eat a simit. Those are two different people.

scroll 04 · passport

The verb in its passport: -mak or -mek

In the dictionary every Turkish verb ends in -mak or -mek. It’s the Turkish “to”: to drink = içmek. Only it comes after the verb. That’s its passport. And that’s how it stands before istiyorum. Not in -yor. In its passport.

-maka · ı · o · u
-meke · i · ö · ü

Look at the last vowel of the root. Back vowel — the -mak door. Front vowel — the -mek door. Same harmony you already know. The root is what’s left when you take the door off.

scroll 05 · assembly

Assembly in four steps

Hit “Play.” Or flip through the steps yourself. The player won’t be offended. It’s seen slower students.

Take the action in its passport. To drink — içmek.

Take “to want” — iste-. Add -iyor. The e hides: istiyor.

Add the person. I — -um. You get istiyorum.

Line them up. Action in front. “Want” at the tail. Done.

Six persons — one seal

The first verb never changes. Only the tail on istiyor does.

scroll 06 · order

English, in a mirror

In English: I want to drink tea. “Want” comes almost first. Turkish does the opposite. Tea first. Then drink. Then want. The verb waits for the very end.

I want to drink tea

A Turkish phrase is like a train. The cars with the action go first. The “want” locomotive pushes them from behind.

Now read the English backwards: tea — drink — to — want — I. That’s Turkish, piece by piece: çay iç-mek istiyor-um. Even “to” and “I” have a place: -mek and -um.

scroll 07 · English ↔ Turkish

Who wants? The tail tells

In English the word I tells who wants. In Turkish the tail does it: istiyorum. That’s why Ben is optional. Pick a person and watch the job move.

1

-mak = “to”

to drink = içmek. English puts “to” in front of the verb. Turkish glues it to the end.

2

He wants → O istiyor

English adds -s only for he and she. Turkish is the opposite: every person gets a tail — except o. One o = he, she and it.

3

No “do”

English needs “do” for “don’t” and “Do you…?” Turkish has no helper. “Not” goes inside: istemiyorum. A question just adds mu: istiyor musun?

4

Say it

ç = ch in “chair”: içmek. -mak rhymes with “mock.” istiyorum sounds like “is-tee-yo-room.”

scroll 08 · yes, no, question

Yes, no, question

No “don’t,” no “do.” The “not” lives in istemek. You don’t touch the first verb. It’s in its passport, it has immunity.

A question is a separate word, mu. No flipping like “Do you…?” It goes after istiyor and takes the person for itself. Tap any cell — it’ll say the phrase out loud. Swimming is just an example. Nobody’s making you swim.

scroll 14 · traps

Six traps

Everyone trips over these. English sets most of them. No shame in it. The shame is a third tea.

Çay içiyorum istiyorum.
Çay içmek istiyorum.

-yor is like “-ing.” You don’t say “I want drinking tea.” You say “to drink” — and that’s içmek.

İstiyorum çay içmek.
Çay içmek istiyorum.

That’s English word for word. Turkish reads it in a mirror: “want” goes to the tail.

Gitmak istiyorum.
Gitmek istiyorum.

The root git has the vowel i. Front. So it’s the -mek door.

Kahve içmek istiyorum değil.
Kahve içmek istemiyorum.

In English “not” is a separate word. değil looks like it, but it doesn’t get along with verbs. The “not” hides inside: iste-mi-yorum.

Beklmek istemiyorum.
Beklemek istemiyorum.

In bekliyorum the e went into hiding. In the passport it comes back: bekle-mek.

İstiyor musun çay içmek?
Çay içmek istiyor musun?

English flips a question: “Do you want…?” Turkish doesn’t flip anything. Same order, mu at the end.
