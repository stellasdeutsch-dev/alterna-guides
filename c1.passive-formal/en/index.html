Rule 1 · four layers

The verb joins the civil service

In the news and in documents, the verb doesn’t walk around in a T-shirt. It puts on four layers.

  1. yap to do. There’s someone doing it: yapıyorlar, they’re doing it.
  2. ıl passive. The doer is gone. Who doesn’t matter. What does.
  3. makta process. It’s happening now. Or it happens all the time.
  4. dır stamp. This is official. That’s how news, letters, and papers are written.

Put it back together: yap + ıl + makta + dır. In English that’s “is being carried out,” “is underway,” “is conducted.” Office-speak. But precise.

English does the same thing. “They’re building it” is kitchen talk. “It’s being built” is the news. “Construction is underway” goes on a form with a stamp. Turkish does it with one long word.

Rule 2 · which ending

Form workshop

Voice first. Look at the end of the stem. Three roads.

Then the process. Last vowel a, ı, o, u: -maktadır. Vowel e, i, ö, ü: -mektedir. Two options. That’s it.

A three-question algorithm. What does the stem end in? What’s the last vowel? Which process ending? Answer in order and the word builds itself.

And ‑dır here doesn’t mean “is.” It’s a stamp of certainty. That’s how a document talks, not a person.

    Advice from a survivor

    Four traps

    t turns into d. et → edil.

    Wrong: etilmektedir. Grates on the ear.

    Right: edilmektedir ✓

    This goes for every verb with et. Official texts are full of them.

    The negative hides inside. ‑ma‑ goes before ‑makta.

    Wrong: üretilmektedir değil. Ears bleeding.

    Right: Bu ürün artık üretilmemektedir. ✓

    This product is no longer manufactured.

    Nobody talks like this at home. Çay demlenmektedir.

    Sounds like a press release about tea.

    At home: Çay demleniyor. ✓

    devam etmek takes -A. In the passive too.

    Wrong: Müzenin yapımı devam edilmektedir.

    Right: Müzenin yapımına devam edilmektedir. ✓
