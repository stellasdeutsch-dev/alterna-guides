Dokuza çeyrek var — “there's a quarter left until nine.” That's 8:45.

One letter and one word. Half an hour of difference. In English the warning word is “to”: a quarter to nine. In Turkish there is no such word. The warning is one letter on the hour, dokuza, and var at the very end. By the end of the series you'll hear var before the numbers.

Episode 2On the hour, half past, day and night

This part is easy

On the hour — just the number. Saat üç. Three o'clock. That's it.

Half — buçuk. Saat üç buçuk. Half past three.

Morning or evening? In conversation, Turks count to twelve. If it's unclear, they put a word before the time. Akşam saat sekiz. Eight in the evening.

Episode 3geçiyor · var

There are 2 ways

Up to half past, Turks count how much has passed. After half past — how much is left. Just like on exam morning.

Way 1 · geçiyor

The minutes have already passed. The hour gets -ı / -i / -u / -ü.

Saat üçü beş geçiyor.

3:05. Word by word: “three · five · is passing.”

Way 2 · var

The minutes are still left. The hour gets -a / -e — “to four.”

Saat dörde beş var.

3:55. Word by word: “to four · five · there are.”

Good news: the logic is English. Five past three, five to four. Same split at :30, same “next hour” after it. The bad news is the order and the missing words. There is no “past” and no “to” — just endings and a verb at the end. Next block.

Build the phrase yourself, step by step. Switch between 3:05 and 3:55 and watch which hour takes the ending.

Special episode · English ↔ Turkish

Same idea, new order

English goes minutes → past/to → hour. Turkish goes hour → minutes → verb. The words “past”, “to” and “at” don't exist here. Endings do their job. Pick a time and watch the words move.

English

Turkish · numbers show where each word came from

Same as English: 12-hour talk, a split at :30, the next hour after it, a quarter = çeyrek. Like “a.m./p.m.”, a word for part of the day: akşam sekiz.
No “past”, no “to”. The hour gets an ending: üçü for past, dörde for to. The verb closes the sentence: geçiyor (“is passing”) or var (“there are”).
Say it right: ç = ch in church (üç, geçe, çeyrek). ş = sh (beş). ı = the a in about (altı). ğ is silent, it stretches the vowel: öğlen ≈ “ö-len”.

Spin the hand

With a finger or a mouse, right on the clock face. The Turkish builds itself. Pink — past, green — left. Drag the hand across :30 and watch the hour jump ahead while geçiyor switches to var.

Episode 4Evidence

The lineup

Twelve numbers. Each has three forms. Four of them act strange. Tap a form — you'll hear it.

You already know the endings: vowel harmony decides which letter goes in. After dokuz — u and a, after beş — i and e. The catch is only with the four suspects.

Four English traps

These come from thinking in English. Tap a card — you'll hear the right version.

dört is stubborn: dördü, dörde. Before a vowel, t becomes d. But in dörtte a consonant follows — so t stays.
Turks don't like two vowels side by side. They insert a y: ikiyi, ikiye · altıyı, altıya · yediyi, yediye.
After the voiceless ç, ş, t — it's no longer -de but -te: üçte, beşte, dörtte. In on bir the last word takes the ending: on biri.
Episode 5Saat kaçta?

At what time?

Saat kaç — “what time is it.” Saat kaçta — “at what time.” Two letters of difference. Tap a row — it flips.

English just adds “at” in front. Turkish has no word for “at” — it changes the end. The rule in one line: geçiyor becomes geçe, var becomes kala (from kalmak, “to remain”). On the hour and half past get -da/-de: üçte, buçukta. The question is just as short.
