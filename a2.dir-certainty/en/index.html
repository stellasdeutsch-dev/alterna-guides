predicate+-DIr=“probably”

English adds a word: “probably”, or “must be”. Turkish adds a tail. Three letters, and you are honest: I didn't see it.

Without the tail, you stand behind every word. With it, you honestly say: I didn't see it. Works with any predicate — where, what kind, who.

Ayşe hastadır. Can you see Ayşe?

Verbs

The tail hooks onto a tense

The tail won't stick to a bare verb. Tense first: -yor, -mış, -acak. Then the tail.

Now, before, later — one tail. Only the tense before it changes. Tap a stop and the dot will move.

uyuyordur

Ali şimdi uyuyordur.

Ali is probably asleep right now.

  • -yor ends in o — the tail is always -dur.
  • -mış ends in ş — the tail is voiceless: -tır, -tir, -tur, -tür.
  • -acak ends in k — the tail is -tır or -tir. K doesn't soften.
  • “Not” goes before the tense: gelmemiştir. With a noun — değildir.
  • English helps here: “must have come”, not “must came”. Turkish too: gelmiştir, not geldidir.

Remember the chain: verb, “not,” tense, tail. The order never changes.

“He has probably left already.” How do you say it?

Compare

English ↔ Turkish

In English a guess is a separate word. In Turkish it's a tail at the end.

English puts “probably” at the start or in the middle. Turkish hides it at the very end. So listen to the sentence down to the last syllable.

Ali is probably home.

The second face

The other side of the sticker

A sign, the news, a rule. Here the tail doesn't guess. Here it sets a stamp.

On a sign the tail sounds as if the sultan approved it personally. News, instructions, a geography textbook — same stamp.

English has no stamp. A sign just says “is”: Smoking is forbidden. Turkish adds the tail: Sigara yasaktır. Don't read it as “probably forbidden”.

One form — two circles. Where they overlap — the tail itself.

How to tell them apart? By the place. A chat about a friend — a guess. A sign on a door — the law.

On the pharmacy door: Eczane 24 saat açıktır. This is…
