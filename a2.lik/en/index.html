02 Story

Wanted glasses. Asked for an eye.

You’re in Izmir. Alsancak, an optician’s. Yesterday on the waterfront you sat on your glasses. It crunched loudly, in Italian.

You say: Göz istiyorum.

The clerk looks at you. Says: Göz mü?

You say: Evet, göz. You point at your eyes. Both, to be convincing.

The clerk slowly backs toward the door. People in Izmir are kind, but they don’t sell organs here.

You were one tail short. The tail is called -lük.

The glasses turned up in your pocket later, by the way. In one piece.

“I want an eye.”
“An eye?”
“I want glasses.” That’s what you meant.

04 Rule

Four steps to a new word

Take a word. Look at the last vowel. Pick the tail. Stick it on. Done, you’re a word builder.

The tail -lIk takes its vowel by harmony: ö gives ü.

Done: gözlük — glasses. A thing for the eyes.

07 English and Turkish

English has a dozen tails. Turkish has one.

English picks a tail by mood: beauty, friendship, sickness, salt shaker. Turkish is more boring and more honest. Always one tail.

And gözlük in English is a completely different word. Eye and glasses aren’t related. In Turkish they are.
