01Story

The street that doesn't lead out

You're in Ankara. Looking for a café in Hamamönü. The GPS runs into a wall.

You ask a passerby. He says, orası çıkmaz sokak.

You translate in your head. Çıkmaz is “doesn't go out.” Sokak is “street.”

A street that doesn't go out. Like a cat that isn't allowed outside.

You say, go out where? He looks at you for a long time. Says, hiçbir yere.

Nowhere. It's a dead end. The verb froze and turned into a dictionary word.

You walk back. The GPS is quiet. It's embarrassed too.

03The big idea

It was a process, now it's a trait

A regular participle remembers who and when. Okuduğum kitap is the book I'm reading.

A participle adjective forgot everything. No person, no moment. Only the property is left. Okunası kitap is a book worth reading. For anyone. Always.

!Verb + suffix with no person = a permanent trait. It goes into the dictionary.

The test is simple. Can you plug in “I,” “you,” “we”? Then the participle is alive. Can't? The trait is frozen.

04Stand 1

-(A/I)r

Always does it

The aorist describes a habit. Frozen, it describes an object's character. Akar su is water that always flows. Not today, but by nature.

Döner kapı always spins. Çalar saat always rings. Even when you don't want it to.

Easy to spot. Before the noun there's a verb with the aorist tail. No person, no date. That's how signs, clocks and doors talk.

05Stand 2

-mAz

Never does it

The negative aorist. The object doesn't do it by nature. Or it can't be done to it.

Kırılmaz cam doesn't break. Su geçirmez saat doesn't let water in. Unutulmaz gün doesn't get forgotten.

There's often a passive before -mAz: kırılmaz, unutulmaz. “Can't be broken.”

One more example: the invisible man. He can never be seen, so it's -mez too.

Glass: kırılmaz cam

06Stand 3

-AsI

Worth it

This suffix sighs: “it would be nice.” Okunası kitap is worth reading. Görülesi yer is worth seeing.

It usually sits on a passive verb: okunası, görülesi, gidilesi.

It has a dark side. Kahrolası is “damn it to hell.” That's how people curse in old movies.

In reviews of books, movies and cafés, this word shows up all the time. It sounds warm and a little bookish.

07Stand 4

-DIk

Never seen before

You know okuduğum kitap. Remove the person and -DIk turns into a trait.

With a negative it means “unheard-of”: görülmedik olay, duyulmadık haber, beklenmedik misafir.

Without a negative it means “familiar, usual”: tanıdık yüz, bildik şarkı.

Hint: there's almost always a passive inside, “by someone.” So the meaning is general: nobody, ever.

08Stand 5

-mIş

Already happened and stayed

The action is over. The result is right in front of you. Kurumuş yaprak is a leaf that dried. Kızarmış ekmek is bread that got toasted.

Like English “dried,” “boiled,” “educated.” Okumuş adam is a man who has read a lot.

And -mIş gave Ankara the word dolmuş. A minibus that “got filled.” Until it fills up, it won't go.

The trait describes the state after the action. Nobody cares about the action itself anymore.

09Bonus

A trait that became a word

Some froze so hard they became nouns. They're often written as one word. Writer, reader, volcano, the past. Flip the card.

10Compare

English does it too

Unseen, unheard-of, unbreakable, unforgettable. These are frozen participles too. English pulls this trick all the time: running water, boiled egg, must-read.

Same mechanism. The action freezes and turns into a property.

16Traps

Five traps

Everyone trips on these. Even people who honestly passed B1.

01

Görmedik is “we didn't see.” You need the passive: görülmedik, “never seen by anyone.”

02

Okunacak is “that will be read,” a plan. Okunası is “worth reading,” a rating.

03

Çıkmayan is just “not going out.” The dictionary has one dead end: çıkmaz sokak.

04

Kurudu is a predicate, “dried up.” Before a noun you need a trait: kurumuş.

05

When a trait becomes a thing, it's written as one word: yanardağ, akarsu.
