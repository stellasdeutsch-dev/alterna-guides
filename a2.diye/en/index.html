formula

First the quote. Then diye. Then the verb

English can already do half of this: “Does it talk?” I asked. Quote first, verb after. Turkish does it every time — and puts diye, “saying,” between the words and the verb: asked, answered, thought.

Q&A: where English speakers slip

Four traps, all from English habits. Tap a question to open the answer.

No. "Merhaba" diye dedi. diye means “saying,” dedi means “said.” Together it's “said saying.” Correct: "Merhaba" dedi.
No. Sordu: "Saat kaç?" That's English word order in Turkish words. Think of “What time is it?” he asked — and push the verb all the way to the end: "Saat kaç?" diye sordu.
No. In English Susanna looks like an object, but Turkish asks to someone: Susanna'ya sordu. You answer “to” someone too: ona cevap verdi.
No. In English “saying” comes before the words. diye comes after them and closes the quote, like a quotation mark: "Merhaba!" diye bağırdı.
