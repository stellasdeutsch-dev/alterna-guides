Day 1 · ferry Karşıyaka → Konak

Forty years and one simit

Morning, 8:10

Izmir. The morning ferry from Karşıyaka to Konak. You’re standing at the rail with a simit.

There’s an old man next to you. He tosses bits to the seagulls. They catch them midair like goalkeepers.

You ask if he does this every day. He says, Her sabah martılara simit atarım. Kırk yıldır. — Every morning I throw simit to the seagulls. For forty years.

You want to keep the conversation going. You say, Ben de simit yiyorum. — I’m eating simit too (right now).

The old man nods. You’re telling the truth: you are eating simit right now.

Only his sentence lasts forty years. Yours lasts until the simit runs out.

Meaning · the flashlight and the sea

Why this tense is wide

The present in -yor is a flashlight. It shines on one spot: now, these days. In English, that’s “I’m eating.”

The aorist is the sea. It’s spread across the whole calendar: yesterday, today, always. In English, that’s plain “I eat.”

That’s why you use it for things that don’t depend on the day of the week. About a person, a city, the world.

Where does the aorist live? In four places.

Markers · frequency words

A calendar of habits

The aorist gets along with frequency words. See her gün or genellikle — and -ar or -ır is almost surely coming next.

But it works without them too. Annem çok güzel yemek yapar is about Mom in general, not about tonight’s dinner. Tap a card — it flips.

Form · three rules

Three fares on one cruise

The aorist ending depends on the verb. Verbs come in three classes — like cabins: Comfort, Premium, Deluxe. Look at the end of the stem and count the syllables. The fare decides the ending, not the price.

Comfort · stem ends in a vowel

Vowel at the end? Just r

If the verb ends in a vowel, it barely needs anything. One r and it’s done. No harmony, no choices. The fare for lazy people.

Short ye and de go here too: yer, der. Short, but they end in a vowel — so they’re Comfort too.

Premium · one syllable

One syllable — -ar or -er

A short verb ending in a consonant: yap, iç, sev. It gets -ar or -er, like the old a/e pair.

And a familiar trick: t between vowels softens to d. git → gider, et → eder.

Turkish has hundreds of these short verbs: on every corner, in every conversation.

Back vowel in the word — -ar: yapar, bakar. Front vowel — -er: içer, sever.

gitmek → o …

Deluxe · two syllables or more

Long verb — -ır

Two syllables or more — the Deluxe fare. The ending is -ır, -ir, -ur, -ür by four-way harmony.

Counting syllables is easy: as many vowels, as many syllables. Tap the syllables — we’ll count together.

A trap for the eyes: kalk looks long, but it has one vowel. So one syllable, and kalkar. Count vowels, not letters.

Crew · who does it

Six members of the crew

The person ending hooks on at the very end. You know these endings from “I am a teacher”: -ım, -sın, -ız, -sınız, -lar. “He/she” has no ending.

The person ending is like a name badge on a crew uniform. One look tells you who’s doing it: me, you, or the whole deck.

English ↔ Turkish · what you already know

English splits it too. Almost.

Good news: English cuts the present the same way. “I’m drinking” is the flashlight: içiyorum. “I drink” is the sea: içerim. Every day, always, in general — that’s your simple present, and that’s the aorist.

The difference is the ending. English marks only he/she, with -s. Turkish marks every person except o. Exactly the other way round. Tap a row.

English
Turkish

Pronouns are optional in Turkish. The ending already says who: içerim alone means “I drink.” Use ben only for contrast: Ben çay içerim — “I drink tea (not coffee).”

Day 2 · a tour of the bay

Izmir lives on a timetable

You’re at Kemeraltı by a boyoz stall. You ask if he’s here every day. The vendor says, Her sabah altıda açarım. — I open at six every morning. You ask who buys it. He says, Bütün İzmir boyoz yer. — All of Izmir eats boyoz.

You ask if he eats boyoz himself. He says, Ben çay içerim. — I drink tea. Makes sense. A chef doesn’t eat his own soup either.

FAQ · common mistakes

Answers to common mistakes

Every other tourist trips on these. The last two are classic English-speaker mistakes. Open a question — inside is the fix and a short “why.”

yapar

One syllable and not one of the thirteen — Premium fare, ending -ar.

gelir

gel is a VIP from the song. One syllable, yet the ending is -ir.

çalışır

Two syllables — Deluxe. Long verbs get -ır, not -ar.

gider

t between vowels softens to d. Like kitap → kitabı.

Ben her gün kahve içerim.

English “I drink” has no ending, so it feels fine. In Turkish, no ending means “he drinks.” “I” needs -im at the end.

Ben her gün kahve içerim.

That’s English order: subject, verb, object. Turkish puts the verb last. Time words like her gün usually go early.

Finale · the ferry again

A week later you’re on the morning ferry again. Same old man, same seagulls.

You say, Ben her sabah bu vapura binerim. — I take this ferry every morning. The old man looks at you closely. He says, Bir haftadır. — For a week now.

A week is a habit too. Small, but yours. The old man smiles and hands you a piece of simit.

By the way, now you throw simit to the seagulls too.
