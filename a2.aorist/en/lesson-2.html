Day 3 · Kemeraltı, noon

Tea you can’t refuse

Noon, 12:40

Kemeraltı. A shop full of copper coffee pots. The vendor puts a glass of tea in front of you. He doesn’t ask — he just puts it there.

He says, Çay içer misiniz? — Would you like some tea? The glass is already there. The question is a formality.

You don’t drink tea after three. You want to say no politely. You say, İçmezim.

The vendor freezes. Like a seagull that missed the simit.

Then he smiles. He says, İçmem. Biz "içmem" deriz. — I don’t drink. We say “içmem.”

You drink the tea. Saying no is awkward now.

Where’s the mistake?

içmezim

Rule 1 · “no”

The negative: -maz / -mez

“No” is -maz or -mez right after the stem. Two-way harmony, like the a/e pair.

No more fares. Even the VIP guests ride like everyone else here: gelmez, almaz, kalmaz. The negative makes everyone equal.

The form is shorter than it looks: stem plus -maz, that’s it. No r, no syllables.

Rule 2 · I and we

Man overboard. Well, z overboard

With “I” and “we,” the z jumps overboard. Without it: gelmem, gelmeyiz.

With “we,” a y steps in between two vowels — like a life ring, so the vowels don’t drown in each other. Everyone else keeps the z.

Crew · all six persons

The crew says no

The whole negative table on one deck. Look at “I” and “we”: no z there. Switch the verb — the rule doesn’t change.

“He/she” has no person ending, same as before. And “they” just add -ler: içmezler.

Rule 3 · questions

The question sails in a separate lifeboat

The question is mi, and it’s written separately. The verb stays on the ship: içer. The person ending moves into the lifeboat: içer misin?

The vowel in mi follows four-way harmony: yapar mı, okur mu, görür mü. Only “they” leave -ler on the ship: içerler mi?

This is how you ask about habits: do you drink coffee, do you get up early, do you speak Turkish.

“Do you speak Turkish?”

English ↔ Turkish · no “do,” no “not”

Fire the helper “do”

English needs a helper for “no” and “?”: don’t, doesn’t, do you…? Turkish has no helper at all. “Not” is a suffix inside the verb. The question is mi, right after the verb, never at the start.

So the English words don’t disappear — they move into the verb. Tap a row and watch where each one lands.

Rule 4 · “no” + “?”

“Won’t you come by?” — gelmez misin

Glue the negative and the question together — and you get a soft invitation. Like English “won’t you have some more tea?”

That’s how people invite you over, for tea, for a walk. You can say no, but politely: içmem, teşekkürler.

Important detail: in a question, the z climbs back on board even for “I” and “we”: gelmez miyim, gelmez miyiz.

Tricky case · t and d

gider, but gitmez

Remember git → gider? t softens to d only before a vowel. In the negative, the stem is followed by m — a consonant. So t stays hard: gitmez, etmez.

The whole family follows one law: eder, but etmez; tadar, but tatmaz. Test it with the button below.

Onboard amenities · meanings in speech

Not just habits

The aorist works in live conversation too. People use it to promise, offer, decide on the spot, and guess the future. Tap the hexagons.

English often uses “will” here: “I’ll call,” “I’ll take it.” Turkish uses the aorist. A handy rule: spontaneous “I’ll…” is -r. But a fixed plan, “I’m going to Ankara tomorrow,” is -yor in both languages: yarın Ankara'ya gidiyorum.

Comparison · the flashlight and the sea

Now or in general

A question about now and a question about life sound different. A friend calls: Çalışıyor musun? — are you busy right now? An acquaintance on the ferry: Hafta sonu çalışır mısın? — do you work on weekends in general?

Same with the negative. Et yemiyorum — I’m not eating meat right now, I’m on a diet. Et yemem — I don’t eat meat, period.

Memory trick · a signal from the bridge

“I and we — z overboard!”

Picture the captain with a megaphone. He shouts one thing: I and we — z overboard! Everyone else — z on board.

Say it out loud three times. The megaphone only works if you shout. A whisper is fine if you’re in a library.

But in the question lifeboat, the z comes back: rescued. That’s why it’s içmem, but içmez miyim?

Day 4 · a yacht trip

A deck where everyone asks

Friends invite you on a trip around the bay. You’re on deck. The bartender asks, Ne içersiniz? — What will you have? You say, Ben çay içerim, kahve içmem. — I drink tea, I don’t drink coffee. The bartender nods. The tea, of course, is already poured.

You ask if they have coffee. The bartender says, Var, ama kaptan içmez. — We do, but the captain doesn’t drink it. Sea logic. No point arguing.

Tap the spots on the deck. Everywhere someone is asking and someone is saying no.

FAQ · common mistakes

Answers to common mistakes

Every vendor in Izmir hears these mistakes. The last two come straight from English. Open one — the fix is inside.

içmem

With “I,” the z is overboard. It comes out short: içmem.

gelmeyiz

With “we,” the z goes too, and a y steps in between the vowels.

gelir misin

The mi lifeboat sails separately. Write it with a space.

gitmez

t softens only before a vowel. Before m it stays t.

İçer misin?

That’s “Do you drink?” copied word by word. Turkish has no “do” at the start: mi comes after the verb and carries “you”: içer misin. And “they” keeps -ler on the verb: içerler mi.

Kahve içmem.

değil is “not” for nouns and adjectives: öğrenci değilim. A verb takes its “not” inside: iç-me-m.

Finale · Kemeraltı again

A week later you’re back in the same shop. The vendor puts down a glass. He says, Çay içer misiniz? — Would you like some tea?

You say, İçmem, teşekkürler. — I don’t, thank you. Clean, no extra z. He nods with respect.

And puts down a second glass. He says, O zaman bunu iç. Bu elma çayı. — Then drink this one. It’s apple tea.

You can turn down tea in Izmir. But only grammatically.
